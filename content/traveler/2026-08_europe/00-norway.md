transaction in a darkened alleyway.

"It's pretty much just Benadryl," Shannon laughs with a roll of her eyes, "but

"How long till I feel something?"

"How long till I get sleepy?"

I am mostly incapable of sleeping on planes. I may nod off for a few minutes
here and there but overnight flights are my nemesis. And tonight is even more
important that I get some shuteye since we won't have a rest day - we land in
Oslo at 12:30 PM and the wedding is at 6 PM that evening in Holmsbu, a small
seaside hamlet about an hour south of Oslo. We didn't plan it that way, the
wedding was originally scheduled for Saturday, but their dream venue opened up
last minute but only for a Friday service.

Our 7:40 PM boarding time comes and goes. My eyelids start to succumb to the
force of gravity. We see passengers still exiting our aircraft 30 minutes past
our scheduled boarding time. The 9 PM flight to Dublin starts to board from the
adjacent gate before our 8:30. The screen at the gate says ON TIME. It's 8:15.
If we were on time we would be verbally acknowledging the exit row requirements.

Finally the last stragglers of our plane exit the bird and my foggy brain longs
for the confines of seat 12D. 5 minutes pass with no announcements. 10 minutes.
Then we see passengers walking across the entrance to our jetbridge on the other
side of the "international arrivals" glass that separates the main terminal from
the walkway to customs. One of the quirks of having a small, easily accessible
airport we are learning is that the international infrastructure is limited.

One international arrivals demarcated zone must service multiple gates, meaning
that if two planes land in adjacent gates both aircraft must fully deplane before
either gate can begin to board. It's 8:45. The gate still says ON TIME. Our
layover in Iceland is 1 hour 15 minutes, a tight turnaround in the best of
situations. We're already 15 minutes tardy and we haven't even begun to board.

At 9 PM, as both gates are finally clear, an IcelandAir representative hops on
the microphone.

"Hello all passengers on flight FI825 to Reykjavik, we are happy to welcome you
on board today, before we begin boarding we need to verify all carry-on luggage
fits in the bin up here by the gate to make sure no bags are oversized."

*are you serious!!??*

telling us about the 

So we spend 20 minutes queued up in a blob of angry travelers going through the
charade of shoving your carryon bag into the metal receptacle. Every 5th person
is carrying a bag that is too large to be stuffed into the unyielding metal
receptacle. No matter how much standing, stomping, squishing, or shoving the
bag just won't fit. Dejected, the traveler must make the walk of shame to the
gate agent counter to fork over a checked bag fee and send the parcel into the
dark underbelly of the aircraft.

As we near the carryon judge, jury, and executioner I'm feeling nervous about
the dimensions of our bags. I have a suit travel bag with all of our wedding
attire and Shannon has her trusty Cotopaxi travel backpack. I'm struggling to
stay awake while doing mental geometry of our bags.

Soon we step to the arbiter of bags and Shannon's bag slides easily into the
slot and I breathe a sigh of relief. My suit bag similarly slides into and out
of the bin and we have passed the final test before hopping on this airplane.

After all that we are finally granted access to the airplane. I splurged for
exit row seats on this leg of the trip in hopes that the extra room will assist
my elusive quest for redeye plane sleep. As we settle in and put our smaller
bags under the seat, the flight attendant swoops by to inform us that we cannot
place anything under the seat in the exit row. Everything must go in the
overhead bin. Shannon hops up to put our bags in the overhead just as everything
goes dark.


*Excuse me, I have a connection I need to make*, a woman exclaims while
barreling down the aisle of passengers unloading their luggage.

I look out the window of seat 12A to see a bus sitting on the tarmac where a
jetbridge typically stands. "Looks like we're all getting on the same bus," I
volley into the aisle with a hint of schadenfreude. She doesn't hear me, eyes
locked on the front of the plane, living in her own little world unaware of the
misfortune about to beset her.

We shuffle off the plane and board the bus, waiting for each and every last
passenger to deplane. I spot pushy woman and practically see steam coming out
of her ears. While we departed nearly an hour and a half late our landing was
only tardy by 20 minutes. Still enough to make our 75 minute layover a little
tighter than I'd prefer but still doable.

We work up a sweat during the 15 minute walk to passport control, where we
choose the *slowest* of the 10 possible lines. We arrive in the middle of the
crowd, but by the time we pass through we're one of the last. According to the
flight schedule boarding began 10 minutes ago so we are 

We hustle to the boarding gate only to find a hefty line of folks gathered at
the gate waiting on a bus. The Reykjavik airport runs at the pace of tarmac
buses. After waiting for 2 busloads of passengers to be whisked away we hop on
the vehicle out to our waiting bird. On the tarmac we can load from either the
front or the back - we're in row 19, which I estimate is on the aftward end of
the aircraft so we hike up the rear stairs to board the back of the plane.

A simple sign "Rows 1-16 front; Rows 17-33 back" would have helped expedite the
boarding process because as we are attempting to move past the rear galley into
the aisle a 60-something white fella is bumping his way down the aisle trailed
by his Asian wife.

"I'm so sorry; Yeah can I squeeze past ya there; Oops, sorry sorry"

Everyone in the last 5 rows smooshes back into the galley while they find their
seats in the very last row of the plane. So they boarded in the front for their
seats in row 33. Really gunking up the works there, Gary.

As we shuffle past he offers another apology with an aw shucks attitude and
Midwestern sensibility,  "I'm so sorry! I shoulda come in on the back here!!"

"No worries!" Shannon replies, just trying to get to our seats.

"Are you Canadian," he asks.

"Huh? No we're American," she says.

"Oh ok, I have only heard 'no worries' from Canadians! I thought you were
Canadian! Very polite people those Canadians..." he continues to tell his wife
about the politeness of Canadians as we make our way down the aisle out of
earshot.
